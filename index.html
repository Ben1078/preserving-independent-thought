Preserving Independent Thought in the Age of AI

Since their introduction, large language models have been rapidly adopted to assist with writing, brainstorming, and research — and while there are clear benefits, early research suggests that societal adoption may pose some unique challenges.

  • An emphasis has been placed on the increase in individual productivity and work quality that these tools can bring
  • Dependence on AI can change creative approaches, cultural expression, personal opinions, and the research topics society chooses to explore
  • These effects are not unavoidable because the way people use AI can help preserve or even increase diversity

Individual benefits and collective costs

While the widespread use of LLMs can enhance the creativity, productivity, and idea generation of individuals, it can simultaneously diminish the diversity of ideas produced when groups of people rely on the same systems to generate ideas.

Individual benefits

  • Increased individual productivity and work quality
  • Stronger stories for writers and faster progress for researchers
  • Improved ratings for novelty, usefulness, writing quality, and enjoyment
  • A competitive advantage that creates a strong incentive for individual adoption

Group costs

  • Many people receive ideas shaped by the same training data and defaults
  • The group's total creative output may become more concentrated around similar themes, structures, and starting ideas
  • Similar ideas that appear useful can replace less obvious alternatives that would otherwise be found
  • Any collective costs that emerge will likely be less visible at the individual level

What the research adds up to

01

The individual benefits are real

The competitive advantage gained from using LLM tools creates a strong incentive for individual adoption. Participants with access to AI ideas improved, on average, in ratings for novelty, usefulness, writing quality, and enjoyment (Doshi & Hauser, 2024), and scientists associated with AI-augmented research published more papers, received more citations, and moved into project-leadership roles earlier (Hao et al., 2026). A writer or researcher who sees immediate improvements in work quality and speed may be heavily motivated to adopt the technology.

02

Groups relying on the same systems produce more similar ideas, and the influence is hard to notice

AI-assisted stories received better evaluations, but they were also more similar to one another and to the AI ideas provided (Doshi & Hauser, 2024). Writing became more similar across cultural groups when participants had AI assistance, and Indian participants' writing moved closer to American styles more than the reverse (Agarwal et al., 2025). Participants given intentionally biased suggestions reported attitudes that had shifted toward the position favored by the AI, and most did not realize they had been influenced (Williams-Ceci et al., 2026). AI-augmented research also covered a smaller area of scientific topics (Hao et al., 2026). Large numbers of users may receive similar ideas that appear useful but replace less obvious alternatives that would otherwise be found (Kleinberg & Raghavan, 2021).

03

The narrowing is conditional and depends on how the tools are used

One study mixed AI ideas into a larger stream of human ideas, and high exposure to those ideas increased both the overall diversity of the idea pool and the rate at which that diversity grew (Ashkinaze et al., 2025). This suggests that homogenization is conditional. AI may narrow thinking when it is used as a starting point or relied upon too heavily, but it may even increase diversity when it is used to introduce unusual possibilities during brainstorming.

The six sources in detail: methods, findings, and limitations

Theme 1 · Writing & creativity

293 writers · 600 evaluators

AI ideas improved stories while reducing their variety

Doshi and Hauser (2024) studied 293 participants who wrote short stories with either no AI ideas, access to one GPT-4 idea, or access to as many as five.

  • Participants with access to AI ideas improved, on average, in ratings for novelty, usefulness, writing quality, and enjoyment
  • The largest of these benefits went to writers who had initially scored lower on the study's creativity measure
  • The stories were also more similar to one another and to the AI ideas provided. The researchers concluded that "generative AI–enabled stories are more similar to each other than stories by humans alone"
  • Limitation: The experiment focused on short fiction and one kind of evaluation, so it does not establish that all AI-assisted writing converges.

118 writers · India & the US

Cultural expression converged toward a dominant style

Agarwal et al. (2025) conducted an experiment with 118 American and Indian participants completing culturally grounded writing tasks with or without LLM autocomplete suggestions from GPT-4o.

  • Writing became more similar across the two cultural groups, with cross-cultural similarity increasing from 0.48 without AI to 0.54 with AI
  • The direction of the convergence was unequal: Indian participants' writing moved closer to American styles more than American writing moved toward Indian styles
  • Changes in cultural references, phrasing, and perspective could not be explained by grammar corrections alone. If AI guides users toward a dominant style, fewer cultural approaches and unique ideas may appear in the larger conversation
  • Limitation: The study used a small sample, two countries, and one model, so the result should not be generalized to every culture or tool.

Theme 2 · Influence & attention

2,582 participants · 2 experiments

Biased suggestions shifted opinions without users noticing

Williams-Ceci et al. (2026) conducted two experiments with 2,582 participants who were instructed to write about social issues, some with autocomplete suggestions containing intentional bias.

  • Participants who were given the suggestions reported attitudes that had shifted toward the position favored by the AI
  • Most did not realize the suggestions were biased and did not believe the suggestions had influenced them
  • Even when participants were warned of the AI's bias, they still experienced a similar shift
  • Limitation: The bias was intentionally one-sided, so the study shows this influence is possible without proving that ordinary assistance always persuades.

41.3 million papers · 1980–2025

More individual impact, narrower collective focus

Hao et al. (2026) used a language model to identify AI-augmented work across 41.3 million natural-science papers published between 1980 and 2025.

  • Scientists associated with AI-augmented research published more papers, received more citations, and moved into project-leadership roles earlier
  • AI-augmented research covered a 4.63 percent smaller area of scientific topics and was associated with 22 percent less follow-on engagement from other researchers
  • The authors concluded that AI tends to favor data-rich areas where its tools are easiest to apply. If research increasingly focuses on established questions, society may lose discoveries that only emerge from less established subjects
  • Limitation: The findings are observational and cover AI tools broadly. They do not focus on generative writing alone.

Theme 3 · Groups & shared systems

844 participants · 48 countries

Homogenization depends on how the tool is used

Ashkinaze et al. (2025) studied 844 participants from 48 countries who completed variations of the Alternate Uses Task after seeing examples created by AI or by earlier participants.

  • High exposure to AI ideas increased both the overall diversity of the idea pool and the rate at which that diversity grew
  • Surprisingly, it did not improve the creativity of individual ideas
  • Rather than relying on a single AI-generated starting point or continuous autocomplete suggestions, the study mixed AI ideas into a larger stream of human ideas
  • Limitation: This was one short brainstorming task with a volunteer sample. It shows that homogenization is conditional.

Theoretical model · "algorithmic monoculture"

Even accurate shared tools can leave options overlooked

Kleinberg and Raghavan's (2021) mathematical model of "algorithmic monoculture" examines situations in which multiple decision makers use the same ranking algorithm.

  • Even when the algorithm is more accurate than independent alternatives, the overall quality of the decisions made can be lower
  • Everyone is directed toward the same highly ranked options, leaving other suitable options overlooked
  • Individual creativity and independent judgment have collective value partly because different people are more likely to notice different possibilities
  • Limitation: This is a formal model of a mechanism. It is not a measurement of any specific AI product.

Widespread adoption of these tools can make creative work more alike, concentrate cultural expression around dominant norms, influence users' beliefs, and overconcentrate scientific attention in already-established areas. However, these outcomes are not unavoidable. Using AI in ways that preserve independent thought, for example as a resource alongside human ideas, has the potential to preserve or even increase collective diversity.

What you can do: five strategies

These strategies are my own deductions from the research. Each one is meant to make sure that some of the thinking exists and belongs to you before, during, and after the tool is involved.

01

Write down your thinking first

Write for a minute before opening the tool. A first attempt does not need to be good to give you an independent reference point.

02

Ask for contrast

Five answers can still express one underlying assumption. Request approaches that are based on genuinely different values, disciplines, cultures, or objections.

03

Use AI as a critic

Ask what is unclear, unsupported, or missing in your work. Questions and counterarguments help you keep ownership of the draft.

04

Leave time to revise

Reading, selecting, and revising are part of understanding the work. Automating every difficult step can remove chances to develop judgment.

05

Check people and primary sources

Follow citations, compare communities, and ask who is missing from the answer. AI can help locate material, and the sources still need to be checked.

Prompt examples

These prompts give the tool a clearer role by asking it to respond to your work, present alternatives, or help you plan what to verify.

Brainstorming

Make the options meaningfully different

I already have these ideas: [paste your ideas]. Suggest three additional directions that begin from genuinely different assumptions. Explain the assumption behind each direction. Do not rank them or choose for me.

Revising

Ask for diagnosis of the draft

Read my draft and identify two places where my reasoning is unclear or unsupported. Ask me one question about each place. Preserve my wording and do not rewrite the passage.

Researching

Map the search and then verify it

For this research question [your question], suggest search terms, relevant disciplines, and two competing viewpoints. Do not invent sources or citations. I will verify each claim using library databases or primary sources.

The takeaway: As adoption grows, the impact of LLMs will depend partly on how individuals and institutions decide to use them. The greatest risk is that real individual gains may hide a broader reality of collective homogenization. Bring your own starting point, ask for contrast, verify what matters, and let the tool respond to your thinking.

Reflection

My goal for this project was to reformat my research argument into a website useful for college students who use, or are considering using, AI for writing, research, or brainstorming. I chose a website as my medium because it allows me to display main ideas clearly and provide optional depth if a section interested the viewer. I was able to use visual examples, expandable explanations, and short headers to achieve this. The hardest part of the process was deciding how much of my research should be included. Since my original topic depended on research papers I didn't want to make unsubstantiated claims in the site but I also wanted it to be digestible. Experiencing that challenge taught me the importance of deciding what information you bring to your audience, whereas initially I assumed design would be most important.
